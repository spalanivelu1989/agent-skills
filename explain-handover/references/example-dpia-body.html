What this workbook is, and why it exists

Every company that builds software for people leaves a trail of personal data behind it: names in a database, email addresses in a log file, a location ping from a phone. In Europe, that trail is governed by a single law, the General Data Protection Regulation, usually shortened to GDPR. Most of what GDPR demands is ongoing and invisible. But one of its obligations takes the form of a document, and this workbook is a template for writing it.

The document is called a DPIA. The workbook's own title expands it as Data Privacy Impact Assessment; the law itself (Article 35 of GDPR) calls it a Data Protection Impact Assessment. The first paragraph of the Introduction sheet explains the choice of word deliberately: protection is about keeping data secure, while privacy is the wider question of whether you should be holding and using the data at all. A DPIA, in short, is a structured written argument that a project's use of personal data is necessary, proportionate and safe — produced before the system goes live, so that problems are designed out rather than patched later.

The single question it tries to answer

The workbook states its purpose in one line that is worth keeping in mind on every sheet: the DPIA "is designed to answer the question SHOULD we do this?" with regard to processing personal data. It then lists four tests the answer must pass. The processing must be necessary, appropriate, proportionate and justified. Every later sheet is, in some sense, a way of collecting evidence for one of those four words.

A running example we'll use throughout

Abstract rules are hard to picture, so this guide follows one invented project from start to finish. Imagine a team is building an internal staff directory: a web app where employees look up a colleague's name, work phone, work email and employee ID. Now imagine a second version of the same app that differs by one detail — it also shows the live GPS location of field technicians so dispatchers can send the nearest one to a job. The two versions are almost identical as software. As we'll see, the workbook treats them very differently, and the reason why is the whole point of a DPIA.

Why this example and not another

The answers already typed into the workbook (Name, Work Phone, Work Email Address, Username and Employee ID marked "Yes" on sheet 4; every screening answer "No") look like a modest internal, employee-facing system. The staff directory is a hypothetical that matches that pattern. It is an illustration, not a claim about what the real project is.

Terms you'll meet on almost every sheet

A handful of words recur so often that it helps to have them in hand before opening any sheet. Each is explained again, in context, the first time it matters.

Personal data Any information that identifies a living person, directly (a name) or indirectly (an employee ID, an IP address, a location trace).
Data subject The person the data is about. In our directory, every employee listed is a data subject.
Processing Almost anything done to data: collecting, storing, viewing, changing, sharing, combining or deleting it. Simply reading a record counts.
Controller and processor The controller decides why and how data is used and is legally accountable. A processor handles data on the controller's behalf (a cloud host, a payroll firm, a software vendor).
DPO Data Protection Officer: the person inside an organisation responsible for advising on and overseeing GDPR compliance. The workbook also mentions "DP champions" and "data protection resource", which appear to mean local privacy contacts.
Sensitive (special category) data Data GDPR treats as especially risky: ethnicity, religion, politics, union membership, genetics, biometrics, health, sex life or orientation — plus, separately, criminal records.

How the sheets are built, mechanically

Almost every question sheet shares the same layout. Column A holds a question number, column B the question, and the answer goes in the cells to the right (usually C, sometimes D or E). Many answers are drop-down lists rather than free text — most offer Yes / No / Unsure / N/A and start on the placeholder -Select-. Answer cells are also conditionally formatted, meaning Excel recolours them automatically depending on what you choose, so a sheet full of "Unsure" stands out. And nearly every question carries a red-triangle cell comment: hover over the question and a note appears explaining what it means, with examples. If you open the workbook and see bare questions, those comments are where the guidance is.

Some questions are sub-questions, shown without their own number and starting "If so…" or "If not…". The Introduction is explicit that these only need answering when the main question's answer calls for more detail.

Sheet 1 of 9 · "Introduction"

Introduction: the rules of the game

Type — reading only, nothing to fill in Feeds — every other sheet

The first sheet is a numbered set of paragraphs under three headings. It asks nothing of you. Its job is to explain why the exercise exists, when it is mandatory and how to go about it, and it contains several facts that change how you should treat the rest of the file.

Part one: privacy, not just protection

Paragraphs 1 to 5 set out the philosophy. Paragraph 2 ties privacy to the ECHR, the European Convention on Human Rights, whose Article 8 protects "a private and family life". The practical consequence is stated plainly: any intrusion into someone's privacy has to be "fully documented and justified". A DPIA is therefore not only a security checklist; it is a record that the processing obeys GDPR's principles (which sheet 3 covers) and has a lawful basis.

Paragraph 3 gives the most useful summary in the whole workbook: a list of the five ways privacy is typically intruded upon. Collecting too much information. Using it in ways it wasn't intended for. Sharing it with third parties. Not keeping it secure. Keeping it longer than needed. If you remember only one thing from this sheet, remember those five, because almost every later question is a probe for one of them. Paragraph 5 adds that the DPIA should be used to find "the least privacy intrusive method" of achieving the goal — a theme that returns on sheet 2.

Part two: when the law requires a DPIA

Paragraph 6 states the legal basis. Article 35 of GDPR requires a DPIA where processing is likely to present a high risk to the people whose data it is. The sheet also cites "A25", meaning Article 25, data protection by design and by default: the duty to build privacy into a system from the start rather than bolt it on. It warns that supervisory authorities — the national privacy regulators in each EU country — can penalise organisations that don't follow the process.

Paragraph 7 is a time-saver that's easy to miss. A DPIA is not needed for every system: one assessment can cover "a set of similar processing operations which present similar high risks". If an existing DPIA already covers a project, you don't write a new one; you update the existing one to include the new project and record that you did so. Sheet 1's questions 5 and 6 put this rule into practice.

Part three: how to complete it

Paragraphs 8 to 14 are practical guidance. The DPIA should "not be seen as a form filling exercise" but as a prompt for discussion in the project team. Paragraph 8 names the completed document as evidence that "the Husqvarna Group is compliant with GDPR" — the first of many references to Husqvarna. Paragraph 9 estimates 2–4 hours to complete and claims the template covers the full requirements of the European Data Protection Board (the EU body that issues GDPR guidance). Paragraph 10 sets out the screening logic: if screening shows low risk, the full DPIA isn't needed, but the file must still be saved to meet GDPR's record-keeping duty, and compliance with the law is still required regardless.

The next paragraph says that where screening indicates a DPIA, "tabs 1 – 7 should be completed". The paragraph after that is unfinished: "The completed DPIA should be submitted to ? For consideration", with a comment reading "Security-DPO-DP forum? To be clarified." The last paragraph introduces Appendix A as a tool for managing privacy risk during development and roll-out.

An open question written into the file

Nobody has yet decided who reviews a completed DPIA. The submission recipient is literally a question mark. Before investing effort in completing it, find out who the document goes to — the comment suggests a joint Security / DPO / data-protection forum, but that is a proposal, not a decision.

Small inconsistencies you might notice

The paragraph numbers run 1–10, then jump to 13, repeat 13, then 14 (there are no 11 or 12). Paragraph 9 mentions the "EU Data Protection Board"; the body's correct name is the European Data Protection Board. Neither affects meaning, but both hint the template was edited from an earlier version.

Sheet 2 of 9 · "1. Screening Questions"

Screening Questions: do we need a DPIA at all?

Type — project details + 14 scored yes/no questions Decides — whether sheets 2–7 are needed

This is the gatekeeper. Its subtitle says the answers "will be used to determine if a Data Privacy Impact Assessment (DPIA) is required". It has three blocks: who and what the project is, whether an existing DPIA already covers it, and a scored list of risk questions.

Block one: identifying the project (questions 1–5)

Questions 1 to 4 are housekeeping — Project Title, Project/Product Owner, Person completing this questionnaire and Date completed. All four are currently blank. Question 5 asks what type of development this is; its comment explains it wants to know whether it's a new system, an existing one, new functionality added to an existing one, or a service that shares personal data. This matters because the next question only applies to existing systems.

Block two: is it already covered? (question 6)

Question 6 applies paragraph 7 of the Introduction. If this is an existing system, is there already a DPIA covering similar processing and risks? It has a Yes / No / Unsure drop-down and a follow-up asking which DPIA. A note under it spells out the consequence: if the answer is Yes, no new DPIA is required, but the file should be saved as a record and the existing DPIA updated to include this project "to ensure this processing does not impact on current risk mitigations". Both are currently blank.

Block three: the 14 scored screening questions (7–20)

Here is the heart of the sheet. Each question names a known trigger for high privacy risk, and each is answered Yes, No or N/A from a drop-down in column D, with a free-text column E for qualifications. The list closely follows the high-risk criteria that European regulators publish for deciding when a DPIA is needed.

What makes the sheet clever is invisible: column G is hidden, and it holds a weight of 1 or 2 for each question. Cell D26 runs the formula =SUMIF(D12:D25,"Yes",G12:G25), which adds up the weights of every question answered Yes. Note B27 then states the rule: a total of 1 or less "can be seen to be low risk" and may not need a DPIA; 2 or more means "the DPIA should be carried out". The score cell turns colour depending on which side of that line it lands.

The weights encode a judgement. Six triggers (7, 8, 13, 16, 18, 20) are weighted 2, so any one of them on its own pushes the project over the line. The other eight are weighted 1, so it takes two of them together. In plain terms: "we're using biometrics" is enough by itself; "it's a new system" is not, but "a new system that also shares data with a new partner" is.

Return to our staff directory. In its plain version, all fourteen answers are plausibly No and the score is 0: low risk, no full DPIA, but keep the file as evidence. Now add live GPS tracking of technicians. Question 13 (systematic monitoring, weight 2) becomes Yes, and question 14 (evaluation by "movement or geographical location", weight 1) arguably does too. The score jumps to 3. One extra feature, and the project moves from "file the screening and carry on" to "complete sheets 2 to 7".

What is currently filled in

All fourteen screening answers are set to No, so the score in D26 is 0. By the sheet's own rule, the project as described is low risk and a full DPIA is not required. However, the project title, owner, completer, date, question 5 and question 6 are all empty, so there is no record of which project scored zero.

Limits of the scoring

The screening drop-down offers only Yes, No and N/A. There is no "Unsure", and the formula only counts "Yes", so an honest "we don't know yet" can only be recorded as No or N/A — both of which score zero. A cell header (H11) reads "Will hide this column when agreed" and the weights column carries the note "scoring guide. Will hide column when agreed", suggesting the weights themselves were still being agreed.

Interpretation

A zero score with no project details reads like a draft: either the colleague ran a quick first pass, or these "No" answers are the template's defaults. Treat the result as provisional until the identifying fields are filled and someone who knows the project has confirmed each answer.

Sheet 3 of 9 · "2. Context"

Context: what the project is for, and whose data it touches

Type — mostly free text, some drop-downs Feeds — sheet 3 (lawful basis per purpose)

The subtitle asks you to "enter project objectives, description, purposes of processing, business benefits and justification". This sheet is where the project tells its story, and where the necessary, appropriate, proportionate, justified tests from the Introduction first get evidence. Everything on it is currently blank.

Why the project exists (questions 1–2)

Question 1 asks for objectives, business justification and benefits — to the business, to the data subjects and to anyone else. Its comment contains an important balancing rule: where the business benefits but the person doesn't, the business "must clearly show the need and benefit outweighs any privacy intrusion". The example given is staff network monitoring: intrusive for employees, but justified by the legal duty to prevent breaches and fraud — and even then the intrusion must be "minimised as far as possible".

Question 2 asks for each purpose of processing — each separate reason the data is used — as a numbered list. The comment's example is "1. Provision of Services, 2. Customer service surveys, 3. Marketing communications". The numbering matters because sheet 3 asks for a legal justification for each numbered purpose. For our staff directory, the list might be "1. Letting employees contact colleagues" and, in the GPS version, "2. Dispatching the nearest technician".

Whose data, and what could go wrong (questions 3–5)

Question 3 asks who the data subjects are — employees, customers, and so on — with a sub-question on whether they will be consulted. The drop-down offers Yes, No, Unsure, N/A and "Representative consulted" (for example, a works council or staff representative speaking for employees). The comment links consultation to GDPR's requirement to be "open, fair and transparent".

Question 4 asks what harm could come to people if the data were compromised, including "perceived harm". Question 5 asks for the categories of data — contact, financial, employment, health — as a broad summary. Sheet 4 later asks for the specific fields.

Who else is involved (questions 6–8)

Question 6 lists other stakeholders, internal and external, and asks whether they'll be consulted on decisions about personal data. Question 7 asks: will Husqvarna Group be the owner of the data? The comment defines owner as the organisation that "determines the purpose of processing and has legal accountability" — in GDPR language, the controller. If not Husqvarna, the follow-up asks who.

Question 8 asks whether Husqvarna will "engage another organisation to process personal data" — that is, use a processor. Its comment gives GDPR's broad definition of processing (collecting, recording, organising, storing, altering, retrieving, consulting, using, disclosing, combining, restricting, erasing, destroying) and says that if anyone, inside or outside the Group, handles the data on Husqvarna's behalf, the answer is Yes. Follow-ups ask what they'll do (cloud hosting, payroll, training), whether Husqvarna's data-sharing legal agreements will be signed before any data is shared, whether the processor uses sub-contractors (sub-processors, in GDPR terms) — particularly outside Europe — and how the processor will prove those sub-contractors comply.

Interpretation: where Tarento probably fits

If Tarento is building or running a system for Husqvarna, question 8 is likely where Tarento itself appears — as the "other organisation" processing data on Husqvarna's behalf. That would make the questions about contracts and sub-contractors directly about Tarento's own arrangements. Notably, sheet 6 already has "No extranal companies" (sic) typed into a related answer, which would contradict this reading. That tension is worth resolving with your colleague.

Is there a gentler way? (question 9)

The last question asks whether there are "less privacy intrusive ways to achieve this result". Its comment uses a memorable example: if CCTV exists to deter burglars and gather evidence of break-ins, why record staff during office hours? Follow-ups ask what the gentler method is and, if you're not using it, why. For the GPS directory, the honest version of this answer might be: share location only while a technician is on shift and has accepted a job, not all day.

A small technical glitch

Several drop-downs on this sheet have a leading space in their options (" Yes", " No", " Unsure"), while the colour rules test for "Yes" without the space. In practice the answer cells may not recolour as intended on this sheet. It does not affect the answers themselves.

Sheet 4 of 9 · "3. Principles"

Principles: proving the processing is lawful and fair

Type — 22 numbered questions in 7 themed blocks Depends on — purposes listed on sheet 2

GDPR's Article 5 sets out a short list of principles that all processing must obey. This sheet, subtitled "demonstrate how the project will be compliant with GDPR principles and lawful requirements", walks through them one block at a time. It is the longest and most legal sheet in the workbook, and everything on it is unanswered (drop-downs still show -Select-).

Lawfulness (questions 1–3)

GDPR says you may only process personal data if you have a lawful basis: one of a short list of legal reasons. Question 1 asks for the basis for each purpose numbered on sheet 2. The comment narrows the options for Husqvarna to four: consent (the person said yes), contractual (needed to fulfil a contract with them), legal requirement (a law requires it) and legitimate interests (the organisation has a genuine need that, on balance, outweighs the intrusion). Its worked example: "1. Provision of services – contractual. 2. Customer service surveys – legitimate interests. 3. Marketing – consent." It stresses that only one basis should be stated per purpose, and that consent is often not the best one.

Question 2 follows the consent path. GDPR consent must be specific, informed, freely given and a clear indication of the person's wishes, and sub-questions ask how it will be collected, recorded (who, when, in what context, to what), how often it will be reviewed ("consent cannot be considered indefinite") and how people can withdraw it. The withdrawal question introduces a recurring idea: Husqvarna is building a group-wide mechanism for people's rights, and any one system must plug into it rather than act alone, because the person's relationship is with the Group, not with one app.

Question 3 follows the legitimate-interests path. Its comment observes that this basis "is likely to require a DPIA to support the processing" — legitimate interests is, in effect, the organisation saying "trust us, it's worth it", and the DPIA is where it shows its working. Sub-questions ask how much the processing intrudes and whether the business objective outweighs the person's rights, with the reminder that justified intrusion is still "not carte blanche". Our staff directory would probably rest on legitimate interests (employees need to reach each other); the GPS feature would need a much heavier justification here.

Transparency (questions 4–6)

Can the project give people a GDPR-compliant privacy notice — the plain statement telling them what is collected, why, how long it's kept, where it's stored and who it's shared with? Question 5 asks whether the processing will be as "open, fair and transparent" as possible, and what steps beyond the notice will show it (the comment mentions proactively notifying people after a breach). Question 6 covers data obtained from someone other than the person themselves, where GDPR still requires the person to be told.

Purpose limitation (questions 7–9)

Purpose limitation is the rule that data collected for one reason shouldn't quietly be used for another. Question 7 asks whether data will be repurposed, and if so whether the new purpose is "closely related" to the original (if it is, consent may not be needed). Question 8 asks how the system will prevent repurposing in practice, and question 9 how it will avoid vague, "generic" purposes, which the comment says make consent invalid and fail the transparency test.

Data minimisation (questions 10–11)

Data minimisation means collecting only what you need. The comment gives a crisp test: "If you can remove fields of information and continue to process for the purpose you need to, then you have collected excessive data." Sub-questions ask how over-collection will be prevented and, when found, erased — and warn against "just in case" retention. This block is the reasoning behind the field-by-field list on sheet 4.

Accuracy (questions 12–13)

How will data be kept correct and complete, and will corrections flow to every Husqvarna system holding the same data? If data comes from elsewhere — bought, for example — how is it validated, including whether the seller had valid consent to sell it? Question 13 asks whether people can correct their own data.

Data retention (questions 14–15)

GDPR calls this storage limitation: keep data only as long as needed. Question 14 asks whether a clear retention policy exists, whether staff using the system will be told about it, and how compliance will be shown. Question 15 asks what happens at the end of the period — erased, or anonymised (stripped of anything that could identify a person, at which point it's no longer personal data).

Confidentiality and integrity (questions 16–22)

The last block mixes security with data sharing and international transfers. Question 16 asks about strict, role-based access. Question 17 asks a subtle one: how long can the data be unavailable before the people it's about are harmed? The comment is careful to say this is about impact on the person, not on the business, and may justify a faster restore time than the business would otherwise choose.

Questions 18 to 20 cover sharing: how Husqvarna's data-sharing agreements will be built in, what categories of recipients exist (generic labels such as "law enforcement" are acceptable in a privacy notice), and how sharing will be tracked so the Group can answer the question "who did you send my data to?". Questions 21 and 22 cover transfers within the EU and outside it, each asking whether the data stays inside the Group. The final sub-question asks where data goes; for countries with an EU adequacy decision (the European Commission's formal finding that a country's privacy law is as protective as Europe's) you can write "Adequacy agreement". For others, the comment says to refer to your data protection contact. Row 47 links to the European Commission's list of adequacy countries.

Wording to read carefully

Question 21 asks whether data will be transferred or made available "from other EU Member States". The comment ("accessible from someone located in another Member State… stored in a location in another Member State") suggests it means to or in other EU states. Also, GDPR's seventh principle, accountability, has no block of its own; the workbook as a whole is arguably the evidence for it.

Sheet 5 of 9 · "4. Personal Data"

Personal Data: exactly which fields, why, and for how long

Type — source questions + field-by-field inventory Partly filled — yes

Sheet 3 asks the principle; this sheet asks for the evidence. Its subtitle says it should "identify the personal data which is necessary to be collected for the processing and its corresponding retention period". It's the most concrete sheet, and the one your colleague has started.

Where the data comes from and how much (questions 1–5)

Question 1 asks who the data is collected from — the person, or another source, and if so which (a data seller, social media, public sources). Question 2 asks how it's collected: in person or by phone, email, web form, paper or removable media. Question 3 asks how it's stored: paper, digital file, audio, video, image, database. Question 4 asks for the estimated number of people whose data will be processed. Question 5 asks whether growing volumes could make the data more sensitive over time — the problem of aggregation, where enough harmless facts combined let you infer something sensitive. The comment makes a cost argument: it's cheaper to design security in than to retrofit it. All five are blank.

The sensitive data inventory (questions 7–15)

Next comes a checklist of the nine kinds of sensitive data GDPR singles out: racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade-union membership, genetic data, biometric data used to identify someone, health data, sex life or sexual orientation, and criminal history. For each, you answer Yes or No. If Yes, you pick a GDPR justification from a drop-down, write a detailed justification, and give a retention period if it differs from the business's standard policy.

The justification drop-down is worth decoding, because it isn't the same list as the lawful bases on sheet 3. Sensitive data needs an additional condition under GDPR Article 9 on top of a lawful basis, and the options map onto those conditions:

Sheet 6 of 9 · "5. Data Subject Rights"

Data Subject Rights: can people control their own data?

Type — 23 questions across 9 rights Links to — Husqvarna group-wide rights process

GDPR gives people a set of enforceable rights over their data. The sheet opens with "Privacy is about allowing data subjects control over their personal data" and then tests, right by right, whether the system will let them exercise that control. All answers are blank. One theme repeats on nearly every question: a request is made to Husqvarna Group, not to one system, so each system must trigger and cooperate with the Group's central process.

General provisions (questions 1–3)

How will people exercise their rights in this system? Can staff easily search for everything held about one person? The comment notes a genuine tension here: pseudonymisation (splitting data so the identifying part is stored separately) and encryption protect data well but can make it slower to find and hand over. Question 3 asks about redaction — blacking out other people's details, or confidential company information, from a document before releasing it to the requester.

Sheet 7 of 9 · "6. Appropriate Security"

Appropriate Security: how the data will be protected

Type — 7 questions, mixed drop-down and text Partly filled — yes

This sheet targets GDPR Article 32, which requires "appropriate technical and organisational measures" to secure personal data. Its subtitle says it aims to show that confidentiality, integrity and security of processing are met. It's shorter than the others, and it's the one place where the comments read most like a security brief.

Two named techniques (questions 1–2)

Question 1 asks whether pseudonymisation would help. The comment gives the clearest explanation in the workbook: split the data into a small set of identifying details and a larger set that can't identify anyone, then concentrate the strongest protection on the small set. An attacker who reaches only the non-identifying part causes "no impact on the data subject so it would not be a reportable breach". Picture it as keeping the coat-check tickets in a safe while the coats hang in the open: the coats alone don't tell you whose they are.

Question 2 asks about encryption, which the comment says EU regulators consider "one of the most effective ways to protect information"; the project should be able to justify not using it. Each has a follow-up asking for the justification if the answer is no.

Governance and testing (questions 3–5)

How will the project decide what level of security is appropriate? (The comment points to the Husqvarna Group Information Security Standard and the minimum levels in its data-sharing contracts.) Will there be a regular plan to test the measures, and how often? If external companies process the data, how will the project ensure they meet any security requirements beyond the contract baseline?

Personal devices and assets (questions 6–7)

Question 6 asks whether staff can use personal devices or personal email (BYOD, "bring your own device"), and how copying data to them will be prevented — the comment mentions data loss prevention (DLP) tools. Question 7 asks you to outline the software, hardware and network assets the processing relies on; it has three labelled rows but no clear answer cells.

Interpretation: what this sheet would normally hold

In most DPIA templates, the final stage records the outcome: which risks remain, what actions were agreed, the DPO's advice, who approved the residual risk and when, and whether the regulator must be consulted (GDPR Article 36 requires this when a high risk can't be reduced). None of that is in this workbook. If the DPIA is going to a reviewer, expect them to ask for it.

Putting it together

The bigger picture: how to read and use the whole workbook

One argument, told in stages

Taken together, the nine sheets build a single argument. The Introduction sets the bar: processing must be necessary, appropriate, proportionate and justified. Screening decides whether the full argument is needed. Context says why the data is used and whose it is. Principles proves it's lawful and fair, purpose by purpose. Personal Data lists every field and its reason, which is how minimisation is demonstrated. Data Subject Rights shows people keep control. Appropriate Security shows the data is protected. Next Steps was meant to say where the finished argument goes. And Appendix A turns the promises into a tracked list of controls with owners and dates.

How you might use it, step by step

  1. Confirm the project — fill in screening questions 1–5 so the file says which system it's about, and who answered.
  2. Check for an existing DPIA — if one covers similar processing, record it in question 6 and update that DPIA instead.
  3. Re-run the screening honestly — have someone who knows the system confirm each of the 14 answers. Read every cell comment; they define the terms.
  4. If the score stays 0–1 — save the file as the compliance record. The Introduction is explicit that this still matters.
  5. If the score is 2 or more — complete sheets 2 to 6 in order, since each leans on the one before; then set owners, dates and status in Appendix A.
  6. Find out where it goes — ask who reviews it, since the workbook doesn't say.

Observations, ambiguities and gaps

Is this a Tarento document or a Husqvarna one?

The file name says Tarento; every question says Husqvarna Group. The most likely reading is that Husqvarna's template is being completed for a project Tarento delivers, but confirm it, because it determines who the controller and processor are and how screening question 11 and context question 8 should be answered.

Is the "low risk" result real?

On the numbers, yes: all fourteen screening answers are No and the score is 0. But the project isn't named, the screening drop-down can't express "Unsure", and no comments justify the answers. Treat it as a draft until confirmed.

Are the pre-filled answers internally consistent?

Mostly. Sensitive data is No in both screening and sheet 4. But encryption is "N/A", "No external companies" may conflict with a supplier's name on the file, Appendix A marks lawfulness "Risk mitigated" with no lawful basis recorded, and "IP Address: No" is unusual for a web system.

What does the workbook not have that a DPIA reviewer might expect?

A description of data flows (where data travels between systems), a likelihood-and-severity score for each risk rather than a simple implemented/not-implemented status, a record of the DPO's advice, a sign-off of residual risk, and the decision on whether the supervisory authority must be consulted. None of these is present; the Next Steps sheet is where some of them would naturally sit.

Is anything in the template out of date?

Possibly. The file was first created in 2015 and refers to Husqvarna's rights mechanism as "being developed". The adequacy link and transfer rules in sheet 3 reflect EU law at the time of writing; transfer rules have changed since GDPR came in, so check current guidance with the DPO before relying on that block.

Back to the staff directory

The plain staff directory and the GPS version share almost every line of code, and that's precisely why a workbook like this exists. Software is judged by what it does; a DPIA judges what it does to people. In the first version, the workbook asks a few questions, adds up to zero and asks you to save the file. In the second, a single field — where a person is standing, right now — trips two screening questions, flips a line on the data inventory, opens a long argument about legitimate interests and lands a red row in the risk register. The workbook, half filled in, currently describes the first version. Whether the real project is the first version is the question it was built to make someone ask.
