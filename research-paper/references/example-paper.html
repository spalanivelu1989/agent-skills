A guide for business readers

Read this first if you don't work in IT or data protection. It covers the whole argument in plain English, without technical terms, in about ten minutes. The rest of the paper is the detailed evidence behind it.

The problem in one picture

Imagine a company's customer list. One line says Nordbutik AB, which is a business. The next line says Anna Svensson, a private person who buys from the company on account. To the computer system they look identical: both are just "customer name". But under European privacy law only Anna's line is personal data, information about a living person. That gives the company duties towards her that it doesn't have towards Nordbutik.

Now multiply that by twenty years of records, held in tens of thousands of separate lists inside the company's main business software. In most large European firms, that software is SAP, the system that runs finance, sales, purchasing and HR. Nobody can easily say where all the Annas are.

Why the company has to know

Europe's privacy law, the GDPR, gives people rights and puts obligations on companies. Four of them matter most here:

  • Show me my data. Anyone can ask a company for everything it holds about them, and the company normally has a month to answer.
  • Delete my data. People can ask to be erased once the company no longer needs their data.
  • Don't keep it forever. A company must delete personal data when it's no longer needed, even if nobody asks.
  • Keep a record. A company must document what personal data it holds and why.

Every one of these assumes the company knows where the data is. Fines can reach 4% of global annual revenue. One German property company was fined €14.5 million because its old archive system couldn't delete tenants' data (a court later cut the fine to €0.9 million but upheld the breach).

Why now

  • Regulators are checking. In 2025, data-protection regulators across Europe ran a joint campaign on whether companies can actually delete data. Several say it will shape their 2026 inspections.
  • Thousands of companies are changing systems. SAP is retiring its older software, ECC, from 2027. Customers must move to the new version, S/4HANA. Moving systems is like moving house: you have to decide what goes in the boxes. Old customer data that should have been deleted shouldn't be carried into the new system.

What a solution has to do

Solving this takes four steps, and each one depends on the one before:

StepIn plain EnglishIs it already solved?

What Tarento already has

Tarento owns a scanning tool called DEEPSCAN. It runs inside a customer's SAP system and can find every place a given person's name appears. It's a real head start, but it has limits:

  • It works only on the old SAP system, not the new one customers are moving to.
  • It looks mainly for names, not e-mails, phone numbers or bank details.
  • It misses the main customer-name field, which is exactly where Anna sits.
  • It can find data, but it can't tell a person from a company (step 2).
  • It has no published customers or prices.

Tarento also has a ready-made template for the privacy risk assessment regulators expect. It was originally written for a client, though, so Tarento needs permission before reusing it.

Who pays

The data protection officer, the person responsible for privacy, usually wants this work but has a small budget (around US$375k a year at the median, and flat). The real budget sits with the IT department and the system-migration programme. So the most natural sale is a privacy clean-up attached to a migration the customer is already paying for.

The competition in one line each

  • SAP itself provides the tools to delete data, but not to find it in older or customised systems.
  • Specialist vendors (e.g. Silwood, EPI-USE) find the likely places, often free, as a way to sell other services.
  • Big privacy platforms (e.g. Microsoft, OneTrust, BigID) manage privacy programmes but don't understand SAP well.
  • Almost nobody publicly offers to label the actual records and carry out the clean-up. That's the opening.

The recommendation

Go ahead, carefully. Don't try to sell DEEPSCAN as a software product, because basic scanning is being given away. Use it as Tarento's door-opener into paid consulting work:

  1. A fixed-price health check (€15–40k, 2–4 weeks) showing a customer where their personal data really is.
  2. Follow-on clean-up projects: deleting and locking away old data. This is where most of the revenue is.
  3. A migration add-on that leaves unneeded personal data behind when the customer moves to the new SAP system.

Start with Nordic clients in retail, utilities and manufacturing, where Tarento already has relationships.

Abstract

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) obliges every organisation to know what personal data it holds, to keep it no longer than necessary, and to find and delete it on request. In most large European enterprises, the system holding the largest volume of structured personal data is SAP ERP, which has tens of thousands of tables, thousands of company-specific custom tables, and personal data spread through free text, change logs and archives. This paper examines why locating personal data in SAP is hard, what existing tools do and do not solve, and what a complete solution needs. It assesses Tarento's existing assets (the DEEPSCAN scanner, its metadata workbook and a DPIA template) against those requirements, and estimates the market.

The central finding is that discovery, meaning knowing which fields could hold personal data, is largely commoditised and often given away. The value lies in classification at the level of actual data values (telling a private person from a company in the same field) and in remediation (retention, blocking, deletion and minimisation, often during the ECC-to-S/4HANA migration). The serviceable European market for SAP personal-data discovery, classification and remediation is estimated at €70–450 million a year (central case about €200 million). A realistic Tarento share is €1.5–2.5 million a year by year four. The paper recommends a services-led offering with DEEPSCAN as its accelerator, gated by customer interviews and two paid pilots over roughly six months.

Keywords: GDPR; SAP ECC; SAP S/4HANA; personal data discovery; data classification; storage limitation; right to erasure; SAP ILM; data protection impact assessment; data governance; market sizing.

Executive summary

The problem. GDPR gives individuals the right to see their data (Article 15) and to have it erased (Article 17). It also requires organisations to keep personal data no longer than necessary (Article 5(1)(e)), to record what they process (Article 30) and to assess high-risk processing in advance (Article 35) [1]. Every one of these duties presumes the organisation knows where each person's data sits. In SAP ERP it usually doesn't. A typical system has around 90,000 tables and more than 900,000 fields VENDOR [19], plus custom "Z" tables, free-text notes, change logs and archives. Standard field names say nothing about whether a given row describes a person.

Why it matters now. Three forces converge in 2026–2028. Regulators made erasure and retention the subject of their 2025 coordinated enforcement action, with 32 authorities and 764 organisations taking part VERIFIED [2]. Failure to delete old data from legacy systems has been fined: Deutsche Wohnen received €14.5m in 2019, reduced by a Berlin court in 2026 to €0.9m with the breach upheld [3, 4]. And SAP ECC loses mainstream maintenance in 2027 [8], while an estimated 61% of roughly 35,000 ECC customers had not licensed S/4HANA by end-2024 ANALYST [10]. Each migration forces a decision about which historical personal data to carry forward.

What the market already offers. Most tools work on metadata, the dictionary definitions of fields, and stop there. Silwood's Safyr "does not search through text data" [20]; Microsoft Purview's SAP connectors do not classify [29]; SAP's own Information Steward leaves mainstream maintenance on 31 December 2026 [14]. Discovery is frequently free, as a door-opener for masking or migration projects [21, 24].

The solution required. A complete answer has four linked steps: find (discovery), label (value-level classification), document (inventory, records of processing, DPIA) and fix (retention, blocking, deletion, minimisation via SAP ILM and migration). This paper proposes a five-layer framework (Section 9) that adds continuous governance on top.

Tarento's position. Tarento has a working in-SAP scanner (DEEPSCAN), a curated list of 2,554 name-type data elements mapped to 13,420 fields in 9,626 tables, and a nine-sheet DPIA template. But the scanner is ECC-only, covers names (and per the website e-mail), omits the main customer and supplier name field (NAME1), does not classify, and has no public references [47, 50].

Market. The serviceable European market is estimated at €70–450m a year, central case about €200m ESTIMATE. Most of it is remediation services, not discovery software. A base-case Tarento share is €1.5–2.5m a year by year four.

Recommendation

Go, conditionally. Build an SAP personal-data services line with DEEPSCAN as its accelerator rather than a standalone software product. Start with Nordic SAP ECC customers in retail and consumer goods, utilities and manufacturing that have an S/4HANA decision due in 2026–2028. Spend only on 15–20 customer interviews, two paid pilots and the minimum rebuild needed to run them. Commit to a full product only if at least one pilot converts to paid remediation and interviews confirm a buyer with budget.

1  Introduction

1.1 Context

SAP ERP runs the finance, purchasing, sales, logistics and often human-resources processes of a large share of Europe's major companies. EMEA accounted for 46% of SAP's revenue in fiscal 2025 [7]. Because ERP systems record every customer, supplier, employee and contact person a business deals with, they are among the largest stores of structured personal data in any enterprise. They are also among the oldest: many ECC systems hold fifteen to twenty-five years of transactions, and their schemas have been extended with company-specific tables throughout that time.

GDPR, in force since 25 May 2018, did not change SAP. It changed what organisations must be able to say about the data inside it. That gap between legal expectation and technical reality is the subject of this paper.

1.2 Problem statement

Organisations running SAP ERP generally cannot produce, at reasonable cost, a reliable inventory of which tables, fields and records contain personal data, of what category, about whom, and for what purpose. Without that inventory they cannot reliably answer access and erasure requests, enforce retention periods, complete records of processing or DPIAs, or decide which personal data to migrate to S/4HANA.

1.3 Research questions

  1. Why is personal data in SAP ERP hard to locate and classify, and what exactly makes metadata-only approaches insufficient?
  2. What do existing products and services cover, and where are the gaps?
  3. What capabilities must a complete solution have, from discovery through remediation and ongoing governance?
  4. How well do Tarento's existing assets meet those requirements?
  5. Is there demonstrated demand and willingness to pay, from whom, and of what size?
  6. What should Tarento do next, and how should it decide whether to invest further?

1.4 Scope

In scope: SAP ECC 6.0 and SAP S/4HANA (on-premise and private cloud), customers in the EU, EEA and UK, personal data under GDPR Articles 4, 9 and 10, and both software and services business models. Out of scope: SAP SuccessFactors, Ariba and other cloud applications (except where they affect competitors), non-SAP ERP systems, and legal interpretation of specific national retention laws. The paper does not evaluate DEEPSCAN by running it; its assessment rests on the tool's published description and on the metadata workbook that drives it.

2  Background: GDPR obligations and SAP data architecture

2.1 What GDPR requires of an ERP system

GDPR defines personal data as any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (Article 4(1)) [1]. A name is personal data; so is a user ID, an employee number or a customer number linked to an individual. A company's name is not, unless it identifies a person, as with a sole trader trading under their own name. Table 1 lists the obligations that bear most directly on data held in SAP.

Art. 5(1)(e) Storage limitationKeep data in identifiable form no longer than necessary.Requires retention rules per data type, and the ability to block and delete.
Art. 9, Art. 10Special-category and criminal-offence data need a specific legal basis and stronger protection.HR data (health, union membership), and notes in free text, must be identified separately.

Two of these pull in opposite directions. Commercial and tax law in every EU member state require invoices and accounting records to be kept for years, so an erasure request cannot simply delete a customer who has unpaid or recently paid invoices. The correct response is often to block the data, which means hiding it from everyday use while keeping it for auditors, and to delete it automatically when the retention period ends. That distinction between blocking and deletion runs through everything that follows.

2.2 How SAP stores data

SAP ERP stores business data in relational database tables. Its data dictionary (DDIC) describes them: table DD02L lists tables, DD03L lists every field of every table, and DD04T holds the texts of data elements, the reusable field definitions shared across tables. For example, data element NAME1_GP ("Name 1") types the name field in the customer master (KNA1-NAME1) and the supplier master (LFA1-NAME1), while AD_NAME1 types the central address table (ADRC-NAME1).

Beyond these standard, or transparent, tables, personal data also lives in places a dictionary search does not reach directly:

  • Custom tables. Tables built by the customer, with names beginning Z or Y, often named in the local language. No vendor list knows about them in advance.
  • Cluster and pool tables. Older storage formats that pack several logical records into one, used for instance for HR payroll results (PCL2).
  • Long texts. Free-text notes on documents and master records, stored in STXH/STXL in a compressed format.
  • Change documents. Logs of previous field values (CDHDR/CDPOS), which preserve old addresses and names after the master record is corrected.
  • Archives. Data moved out of the live database by SAP's archiving framework but still retrievable, and still subject to GDPR.
  • Non-production copies. Test, training and development systems refreshed from production, which carry the same personal data unless masked.

2.3 ECC, S/4HANA and the migration wave

SAP ECC, the previous ERP generation, receives mainstream maintenance until the end of 2027, with optional extended maintenance to 2030 [8] and a paid transition option to 2033 [9]. S/4HANA, its successor, is maintained to at least 2040 [8]. Gartner estimates about 35,000 ECC customers worldwide, of whom roughly 61% had not licensed S/4HANA by the end of 2024 ANALYST [10, 11]. In a 2025 survey of UK and Irish SAP users, 79% described data management as a challenge in the move SURVEY [13].

S/4HANA changes the personal-data landscape in three relevant ways. Customers and suppliers become business partners (table BUT000), which carry an explicit category: person, organisation or group. SAP's newer data models carry @PersonalData annotations that mark fields as personal [17]. And SAP offers Data Privacy Integration services on its cloud platform [16]. These help for SAP-delivered objects, but they do not cover data still on ECC, custom tables, or free text.

2.4 SAP's own privacy tooling

SAP provides Information Lifecycle Management (ILM) for retention rules, blocking and destruction; Read Access Logging for recording who viewed sensitive fields; and UI masking for hiding values on screen. Licensing for ILM in a GDPR-only context is disputed in practitioner sources [18]. Crucially, ILM acts only on data objects someone has already identified and assigned a retention rule. It is an execution engine, not a discovery tool. SAP's older data-quality product, Information Steward, was positioned for GDPR use after 2017, but its current introduction page does not mention privacy [15] and its mainstream maintenance ends on 31 December 2026 [14].

4  The problem: why personal data in SAP is hard to govern

4.1 A running example

Consider two consecutive rows in the SAP customer master, table KNA1. In field NAME1, the first holds Nordbutik AB, a company. The second holds Anna Svensson, a sole trader who buys spare parts on account. (Both are hypothetical.) The field, its data element and its dictionary description are identical: "Name 1". Under GDPR, only the second row is personal data.

This pair captures the core difficulty. Any tool that reasons from the dictionary alone has to treat KNA1-NAME1 as either personal or not personal for all rows. Tag it "personal" and every business customer floods the inventory; tag it "not personal" and every private customer disappears from it. Tarento's own workbook made the second choice: NAME1_GP and AD_NAME1 are untagged, so KNA1-NAME1, LFA1-NAME1 and ADRC-NAME1 never reach its list of tables to scan [50]. The rest of this paper keeps returning to these two rows, because the question of who can tell them apart cheaply and at scale is, in miniature, the business question.

4.2 Four sources of difficulty

(a) Scale

Silwood reports roughly 90,000 tables and more than 900,000 fields in an SAP ERP system, and a social-security-type identifier present in more than 900 tables VENDOR [19, 20]. Tarento's workbook shows the same pattern from the other side. Starting from SAP's own dictionary, 21,737 data elements mention "name" in some form, and the 2,554 accepted as describing a person are used in 13,420 fields across 9,626 tables [50]. Manual review at that scale is not feasible.

(b) Semantics: metadata does not determine content

The NAME1 example is one instance of a general problem. The same field can hold personal or non-personal data depending on the row; generic fields such as "Text", "Reference" or "Contact" can hold anything; and a correctly named field can be empty in a given system. Only the values themselves settle the question.

(c) Customisation

Custom Z tables are where standard lists fail. In Tarento's workbook, all 36 custom tables have Swedish names, for example ZGRAVERING_NAMN with a field ANVANDARE ("user") [50]. That indicates the extract came from one customer's system and would not carry over to another. Tarento's own web page acknowledges that badly named custom fields are "a gap of course" [47].

(d) Hidden stores and legal tension

Long texts, change documents, cluster tables, archives and non-production copies all hold personal data that a transparent-table search misses (Section 2.2). Even once the data is found, it cannot simply be deleted: accounting records must be kept for statutory periods, so the system must distinguish data to delete, data to block, and data to keep. Getting that wrong either breaks financial integrity or leaves unlawful data in place.

4.4 Evidence that the problem is current

Three strands of evidence show that this is a live problem rather than a 2018 one.

Regulatory focus. The European Data Protection Board's 2025 coordinated enforcement action examined the right to erasure. 32 supervisory authorities and 764 organisations took part, and the report found missing erasure procedures and "difficulty setting retention periods". Several authorities, including the French, Portuguese and Swedish ones, say the findings will shape their 2026 inspections VERIFIED [2].

Enforcement against legacy storage. In 2019 the Berlin authority fined Deutsche Wohnen €14.5m because its archive system could not delete tenant data that was no longer needed [3]. In June 2026 the Berlin Regional Court upheld the breach but reduced the fine to €900,000 VERIFIED [4]. Across all cases, GDPR fines totalled about €1.2bn in 2025 and €7.1bn since 2018 [5]. The CMS Enforcement Tracker counts 3,062 fines worth about €6.1bn, with "insufficient compliance with general data processing principles", which includes storage limitation, the second most-fined category [6].

Migration as a forcing event. Every ECC-to-S/4HANA migration must decide which historical data to move. Personal data that is past its retention period should not be migrated at all, and moving it into a new system arguably compounds the storage-limitation problem ESTIMATE.

Problem in one paragraph

Organisations must be able to find, classify and delete personal data, and regulators are now actively checking that they can. In SAP the data is spread across tens of thousands of tables, custom tables and hidden stores, and the field definitions cannot tell a person from a company. Finding where personal data could be is solved well enough; knowing where it actually is, and then removing it safely without breaking accounting records, is not.

5  Existing approaches and the competitive landscape

5.1 A taxonomy of approaches

Existing offerings fall into four groups:

  1. SAP-native tooling. Information Steward, ILM and blocking, Data Privacy Integration and Business Data Cloud annotations. These are authoritative for SAP-delivered objects but weak on ECC custom content, and in Information Steward's case near end of mainstream maintenance.
  2. SAP metadata specialists. Silwood Safyr, which reads SAP's dictionary in depth and feeds catalogues such as Collibra, Informatica and erwin [19, 22].
  3. SAP data-management and masking vendors. EPI-USE Labs, Natuvion, Libelle, SNP, Delphix and others, who discover personal data mainly in order to mask or transform it [23–28].
  4. Cross-platform privacy and catalogue platforms. Microsoft Purview, BigID, OneTrust, Securiti, IBM Guardium, Collibra and Atlan. These own the privacy programme or catalogue, but have little SAP application context [29–33].

5.3 The gap

No vendor reviewed publicly documents value-level classification across custom Z tables, payroll cluster tables, long texts, change documents and archives, combined with delivery of the resulting remediation. This is an absence of evidence, not proof of a gap; competitors may do more than they publish, and the pilots in Section 12 should test it. But it is consistent with the pattern in Table 2: metadata specialists stop at the dictionary, privacy platforms stop at the database layer without SAP meaning, and SAP-native tools cover SAP's own objects.

Two positions are therefore open to a new entrant. The weak one is "another discovery tool", competing with free offers and with platforms customers already own. The defensible one is "find it, prove it, fix it" for SAP: a value-aware scanner that separates Nordbutik's row from Anna's, reaches custom and hidden stores, writes its output straight into a DPIA, records of processing and ILM retention rules, and is backed by consultants who carry out the deletion, ideally inside an S/4HANA migration. Under that position, the catalogue and privacy platforms become channels rather than rivals.

6  Tarento's current assets: a technical assessment

6.1 The name-field workbook (DEEPSCAN's metadata store)

The workbook was created in September 2018 and last edited in October 2026. Its columns correspond to SAP dictionary tables: ROLLNAME and DDTEXT from DD04T, and TABNAME, FIELDNAME, KEYFLAG and DOMNAME from DD03L [50]. Tarento's web page describes exactly this method: filter DD04T by keyword, then find the tables using the matching data elements [47]. The workbook is therefore, by inference, DEEPSCAN's "GDPR metadata repository" or a direct ancestor of it.

The workbook demonstrates a repeatable method for reducing SAP's twenty-thousand-odd name-like definitions to a curated list and then to the tables that need checking. The Mustard and Not Used tags show that human judgement was applied. The detailed profile, however, reveals five weaknesses:

  1. The most important name fields are missing. NAME1_GP and AD_NAME1 are untagged, so the main name of every customer, supplier and address is excluded (Section 4.1).
  2. 87% of fields are user IDs. 11,674 of the 13,420 fields are 12-character SAP user IDs such as ERNAM ("created by") and AENAM ("changed by"). These are personal data about employees, but low-risk. Only about 1,900 fields in about 1,120 tables hold an actual personal name.
  3. Names only. There is no e-mail (AD_SMTPADR), birth date (GBDAT), bank or IBAN, tax number, telephone or street address.
  4. Single-system custom tables. The 36 Z tables are Swedish-named and evidently come from one customer's system.
  5. No traceability. The tables sheet does not record each field's data element, so a reviewer cannot see why a field was included.

6.2 The DPIA questionnaire

The second workbook is a nine-sheet DPIA template [51]. It contains 14 weighted screening questions, where a score of 2 or more triggers a full DPIA; sheets on context, principles, a personal-data category checklist, data-subject rights and security; and a 13-row privacy risk register graded Yes, Partial or No. Two parts are directly reusable. Its category checklist, from ordinary identifiers to Article 9 special categories, is a ready-made classification taxonomy. And its screening and risk sheets are the natural destination for scan results: "these tables hold these categories, therefore these risks apply".

Constraint on reuse. Every question names Husqvarna Group rather than Tarento; the submission route is marked "to be clarified"; the Next Steps sheet is a placeholder; and all screening answers are "No". It should be treated as a client-derived template. Tarento needs to confirm it has the right to reuse it, then de-brand and complete it before it goes into any offering.

6.3 DEEPSCAN as described

According to Tarento's web page VENDOR [47], DEEPSCAN is an ABAP report run from transaction SE38 inside the customer's SAP system, needing "no extra servers". The user selects an attribute, for example a user ID or name, and enters a value. The tool lists every standard and Z table containing that value, with row counts, field names and primary keys; results can be exported and the run can be scheduled in the background. The page is unusually candid about its limits: free-text fields are not covered; badly named custom fields are "a gap of course"; special categories are "beyond the purview of the tool"; only transparent tables and character fields are scanned; and S/4HANA and HR are "future scope". No customers, prices or version dates are published.

9  What a complete solution requires

The preceding sections establish what is missing. This section specifies, independently of any vendor, what a complete solution for personal data in SAP must contain. It is the reference against which Tarento's (or anyone's) offering should be measured.

9.1 A five-layer framework

L1 Discover
Metadata map of candidate personal-data fields across standard, custom (Z/Y), cluster, long-text, change-document and archive stores, on both ECC and S/4HANA.
L2 Classify
Value-level sampling and rules decide whether each field, and where needed each row, holds personal data; assign category (ordinary, Art. 9, Art. 10), data-subject type (customer, supplier contact, employee) and sensitivity.
L3 Document
Inventory linked to purposes and legal bases; generated RoPA entries (Art. 30); DPIA inputs (Art. 35); exports to privacy platforms and catalogues.
L4 Remediate
Retention rules and residence times in ILM; blocking of data under legal hold; deletion and archive clean-up; masking of non-production copies; minimisation in S/4HANA migration.
L5 Sustain
Scheduled rescans; change control for new fields and Z tables; DSAR and erasure handling; evidence for audits; KPIs.
Cross-cutting governance: ownership (DPO, data owners, SAP CoE), policies, retention schedule, risk acceptance, security of the tooling itself (read-only, certified, logged).

9.3 Non-functional requirements

  • Safety in production: read-only by default; resource-limited background execution; no data leaves the system unless explicitly exported.
  • Trust: SAP-certified add-on with a registered namespace; independent security review; a data-processing agreement, since the tooling processes personal data.
  • Honesty about coverage: results state what was scanned, what was not, and confidence. A tool should never claim an inventory is "complete"; doing so creates liability when it is wrong.
  • Portability: rule packs built from a clean reference SAP system, not from one customer's extract.

12.2 Interview questions

  1. Has an access or erasure request, an audit or a DPIA ever stalled because nobody could say where a person's data sits in SAP? (Tests experienced pain, not legal theory.)
  2. Who paid the last time you did SAP data-privacy work: the privacy budget, IT, or a migration programme? How much? (Finds the real buyer and a real price.)

12.3 Pilot success metrics

  • Personal-data fields and tables found versus a dictionary-only baseline (e.g. Safyr or a keyword search), including Z tables.
  • Precision and recall of person-or-company classification on NAME1-type fields, against a hand-labelled sample.
  • Time from scan to a usable RoPA extract and DPIA draft.
  • Whether the customer commissions remediation: the key commercial signal.
  • Security and performance acceptance of running the tool in production.

Decision rule

Invest in Phase 3 only if (a) at least one pilot converts to paid remediation and (b) interviews identify a buyer with budget. If customers want only the scan, keep DEEPSCAN as an internal accelerator and stop product investment: in that case it is a feature that competitors already give away.

13  Discussion and open questions

What the evidence shows. The legal pressure is real and current; the technical problem is genuine and well defined; competitors cluster at metadata discovery and masking; and observable spending concentrates on remediation and migration.

What remains uncertain. First, the size of the SAP-specific market: no public figure exists, and the bottom-up estimate rests on assumptions about customer counts and spend that could each be off by half. Second, whether competitors already offer value-level classification privately; their silence in public documentation is suggestive, not conclusive. Third, the pace of SAP's own tooling: if Business Data Cloud annotations and Data Privacy Integration extend to ECC and custom content, part of the gap closes. Fourth, the extent to which reduced fines on appeal, as in Deutsche Wohnen, temper buyers' sense of urgency.

A cautious view. A sceptic would argue that GDPR has been in force for eight years, that most large SAP customers have already "done GDPR" with SAP ILM and a consultancy, and that what remains is a long tail of small projects. The 2025 EDPB findings on missing erasure procedures argue against the first half of that view; the absence of published prices for SAP discovery is consistent with the second. The interviews in Section 12 are designed to settle it.

The author's judgement. The strongest argument for proceeding is not the size of the market but the fit: Tarento already has a scanner that runs where the data is, a method for curating SAP metadata, a DPIA template and a Nordic customer base. The strongest argument for caution is that none of these, on their own, is something customers have shown they will pay for.

14  Conclusions and recommendations

  1. The problem is precise. SAP's field definitions cannot tell a private person from a company in the same field, and much personal data lives outside standard tables. Organisations must nonetheless find, classify and delete it, and regulators are checking.
  2. Discovery is commoditised; classification and remediation are not. Value is in reading the data itself and in delivering the deletion safely.
  3. A complete solution has five layers: discover, classify, document, remediate and sustain, with governance across all of them (Section 9).
  4. Tarento's assets are a credible start but not a product. DEEPSCAN needs S/4HANA support, more attribute types, value-level classification, access to hidden stores, integrations and certification.
  5. The market is a niche, not a moonshot: SAM about €200m a year in Europe; Tarento base case €1.5–2.5m by year four.
  6. Recommendation: pursue a services-led SAP privacy offering with DEEPSCAN as accelerator; Nordic ECC customers in retail, utilities and manufacturing first; gate investment on interviews and paid pilots as set out in Section 12.

Return, finally, to the two rows in KNA1. Every tool on the market can find the field that holds Nordbutik AB and Anna Svensson. Few can say which row is the person, and fewer still will then delete her outdated record without breaking the accounting history. If Tarento can do all three in one engagement, it has a business; if it can only find the field, it has a feature that others already give away.

Appendix A: Glossary

TermMeaning
ABAP / SE38SAP's programming language; SE38 is the transaction used to run an ABAP report. DEEPSCAN runs this way.
BlockingRestricting personal data from everyday use while it must still be retained for legal reasons; only authorised roles (e.g. auditors) can see it.
Business partnerS/4HANA's unified master-data object for customers, suppliers and contacts (table BUT000), with an explicit category of person, organisation or group.
Change documentsSAP's log of previous field values (CDHDR/CDPOS); retains old names and addresses.
ClassificationDeciding whether data is personal, of what category, about whom, and how sensitive.
Cluster tableLegacy SAP storage that packs several logical records into one (e.g. payroll in PCL2).
Controller / processorThe controller decides why and how personal data is used; the processor acts on its instructions (Art. 4, 28).
Data dictionary (DDIC)SAP's catalogue of tables (DD02L), fields (DD03L) and data elements (DD04T).
Data elementA reusable SAP field definition shared by many tables (e.g. NAME1_GP).
DEEPSCANTarento's in-SAP ABAP scanner for locating a person's data.
DiscoveryFinding which tables and fields could hold personal data.
DPIAData Protection Impact Assessment, a structured risk review before high-risk processing (Art. 35).
DPOData Protection Officer: oversees GDPR compliance within an organisation.
DSARData Subject Access Request: a person's request to see their data (Art. 15).
ECC / S/4HANASAP's previous ERP generation (mainstream maintenance to 2027) and its successor.
EDPBEuropean Data Protection Board: the body of EU data-protection regulators.
ILMSAP Information Lifecycle Management: retention rules, blocking and destruction of data.
Long textFree-text notes attached to SAP objects (STXH/STXL).
MaskingReplacing real values with realistic fake ones, typically in test systems.
Metadata vs. valuesMetadata describes data (field name "Name 1"); values are the contents ("Anna Svensson").
RemediationFixing what was found: retention, blocking, deletion, masking, minimisation.
RoPARecord of Processing Activities (Art. 30).
Selective data transitionMigrating to S/4HANA while carrying over only chosen data.
Special-category dataHealth, ethnicity, religion, union membership, biometrics, etc. (Art. 9).
TAM / SAM / SOMTotal, serviceable and obtainable market.
Z tableA customer-built custom table (name starts with Z or Y).
